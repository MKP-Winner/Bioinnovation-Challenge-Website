field notes

What Alzheimer's actually is

The biology in plain terms, before the genetics.

Alzheimer's disease is a progressive brain condition and the most common underlying cause of dementia — though not the only one; vascular dementia, Lewy body dementia and others also exist. It develops gradually, often starting with the buildup of amyloid-beta protein into plaques between neurons, followed by twisted strands of tau protein forming tangles inside them. Over years, this disrupts communication between brain cells and eventually causes the cells to die.

The disease typically moves through a preclinical stage with no outward symptoms, then mild cognitive impairment marked by subtle memory or thinking changes, and finally dementia, where symptoms interfere with daily life. Early changes usually appear in regions tied to memory before spreading to areas that govern language, reasoning and movement.

Age is the strongest overall risk factor, but genetics play a substantial role too — particularly for the rarer, early-onset form of the disease, which can appear before age 65 and is more likely to run in families with a clear inherited pattern.

know the difference

Warning signs vs. typical aging

Occasional forgetfulness is a normal part of getting older. These are the kinds of changes that are usually more significant — paired against what's typical, in general terms.

Possible sign

Memory loss that disrupts daily life — forgetting recently learned information or relying heavily on notes and family members for things once handled independently.

Typical aging

Occasionally forgetting a name or appointment, then recalling it later.

Possible sign

Difficulty planning or solving problems, such as trouble following a familiar recipe or keeping track of monthly bills.

Typical aging

Making an occasional error when balancing a checkbook or budget.

Possible sign

Losing track of dates, seasons, or where they are and how they got there.

Typical aging

Getting briefly confused about the day of the week, then remembering.

Possible sign

New problems finding the right word, or stopping mid-conversation with no idea how to continue.

Typical aging

Sometimes struggling to find the right word, but recovering quickly.

Possible sign

Misplacing items in unusual places and being unable to retrace steps to find them.

Typical aging

Misplacing things occasionally and retracing steps to find them.

Possible sign

Withdrawal from work, hobbies or social activities, or noticeable changes in mood and personality.

Typical aging

Sometimes feeling tired of social obligations or set in one's ways.

how it progresses

The stages of Alzheimer's

A real, ordered progression — though the pace varies a great deal between individuals, and not everyone with early stages goes on to develop later ones.

1

Preclinical Alzheimer's years, often decades, before symptoms

Brain changes such as amyloid buildup can begin long before any noticeable symptoms appear. There's no outward sign at this stage — it's typically identified only through research imaging or biomarker studies.

2

Mild cognitive impairment highly variable, often a few years

Subtle memory or thinking changes become noticeable to the person or their family, but daily independence generally stays intact. Not everyone with MCI goes on to develop Alzheimer's dementia.

3

Mild dementia roughly 2 years, on average

Memory lapses grow more frequent and complex tasks — managing finances, planning an event — become harder, though the person is usually still largely independent.

4

Moderate dementia often the longest stage

Confusion deepens, personality and behavior changes can emerge, and help is typically needed with daily activities like dressing, bathing or preparing meals.

5

Severe dementia typically 1–3 years

The ability to communicate and control movement declines substantially, and around-the-clock care is generally required.

known loci

Genes tied to Alzheimer's risk

Tap a card for a closer read. These are the genes most consistently linked to Alzheimer's in published research — not a checklist for self-diagnosis.

APOE4
chr19q13.32 · APOE ε4 allele
strongest common factor

The APOE gene comes in three common versions — ε2, ε3, ε4. Carrying one or two copies of ε4 is the strongest common genetic risk factor for the typical, late-onset form of Alzheimer's.

One ε4 copy: roughly 2–3× average risk.
Two ε4 copies: roughly 8–12× average risk.
Defined by SNPs rs429358 + rs7412.
Not deterministic — many carriers never develop the disease, and many patients carry no ε4 at all.
+ read more
PSEN1
chr14q24.2 · Presenilin 1
early-onset

The most common known cause of early-onset familial Alzheimer's. Mutations alter how the gamma-secretase complex processes amyloid precursor protein.

Inheritance: autosomal dominant — one mutated copy is generally enough to cause disease.
Typical onset: 30s–50s.
Accounts for the largest share of known familial early-onset cases.
+ read more
PSEN2
chr1q42.13 · Presenilin 2
early-onset

A close relative of PSEN1 with a similar role in amyloid processing. Mutations are rarer and tend to show more variable age of onset.

Inheritance: autosomal dominant, incomplete penetrance in some families.
Onset can range more widely than PSEN1, into the 50s–60s.
Far fewer known families affected worldwide than PSEN1.
+ read more
APP
chr21q21.3 · Amyloid Precursor Protein
early-onset

Encodes the protein that amyloid-beta is cut from. Certain mutations increase production of the forms that clump into plaques.

Inheritance: autosomal dominant.
Sits on chromosome 21 — people with Down syndrome (trisomy 21) carry an extra copy and face markedly higher, earlier risk.
One of the first genes linked to familial Alzheimer's, identified in the late 1980s.
+ read more
TREM2
chr6p21.1 · Myeloid receptor
risk modifier

Involved in how microglia — the brain's immune cells — clear debris and respond to amyloid. Rare variants shift risk without directly causing disease.

Variant of interest: R47H (rs75932628).
Associated with roughly 2–3× increased late-onset risk, similar in scale to a single APOE ε4 copy.
Studied as a target for immune-based therapies.
+ read more
modifiable factors

What research links to lower risk

These factors are associated with lower dementia risk across large population studies. None of them guarantee prevention — especially for the rare, inherited early-onset forms driven by genes like PSEN1 — but they're the levers researchers keep coming back to.

Cardiovascular health

Managing blood pressure, cholesterol and diabetes supports blood flow to the brain.

Physical activity

Regular movement is one of the most consistently studied protective habits.

Balanced diet

Diets rich in vegetables, fish and whole grains are linked to slower cognitive decline.

Quality sleep

Deep sleep helps the brain clear waste proteins, including amyloid-beta.

Social connection

Regular social engagement is associated with better long-term cognitive outcomes.

Mental engagement

Learning, reading and other stimulating activities may help build cognitive reserve.

Hearing health

Untreated hearing loss is one of the largest known modifiable risk factors identified so far.

Limit alcohol & avoid smoking

Both are consistently linked to higher dementia risk in long-term studies.

clinical process

How Alzheimer's is actually diagnosed

The real pipeline behind a legitimate diagnosis — in order, because the order is the point. No single step stands alone.

  1. 01

    Clinical & cognitive evaluation

    A doctor reviews medical history, runs cognitive tests covering memory and language, and talks with family about changes they've noticed.

  2. 02

    Brain imaging

    MRI or CT scans help rule out other causes like strokes or tumors; PET scans can sometimes visualize amyloid or tau buildup directly.

  3. 03

    Biomarker testing

    Cerebrospinal fluid analysis, and increasingly blood-based tests, can measure amyloid and tau protein levels to support a diagnosis.

  4. 04

    Genetic counseling

    When early-onset or strong family history is present, counseling and testing for genes like PSEN1, PSEN2 or APP may be offered — always alongside professional interpretation.
